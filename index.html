01 — Systematic review and meta-analysis · first author

Emotional acceptance and affective experience

With Prof. Maya Tamir (Hebrew University of Jerusalem) and Dr. Benjamin A. Katz (University of Haifa)

Status: preregistered on OSF. Analysis and write-up ongoing; findings not yet public.

Emotional acceptance — allowing an emotion to run its course rather than suppressing it or reframing the situation — sits at the meeting point of clinical and experimental psychology, and is central to acceptance- and mindfulness-based approaches. The experimental evidence, however, is scattered across very different laboratory paradigms and outcome measures. This review asks what brief experimental manipulations of acceptance actually do to affective experience, synthesising the controlled experimental literature systematically rather than narratively.

As first author I designed and ran the search and screening — roughly 10,900 records assessed against predefined eligibility criteria, narrowed to 97 eligible experiments — and developed the coding framework, the effect-size extraction protocol and the analysis plan.

02 — Energy transition · behavioral synthesis · co-author

Social diffusion in residential renewable-energy adoption

With Prof. Itay Fischhendler and Dr. A. Yair Grinberger (Hebrew University of Jerusalem)

Status: manuscript in preparation, not yet submitted; details not public.

Rooftop solar is technically and economically viable across most of the places it could be installed, yet adoption remains strikingly uneven between communities. This study examines that unevenness as a social process unfolding over time — how adoption spreads between neighbouring communities, and which local conditions accompany different adoption trajectories.

My contribution is the theoretical synthesis. I consolidated the literature on social contagion and peer influence in technology adoption — how visibility, network structure, social proximity and group membership govern whether an observed behaviour propagates — and integrated the statistical results with what that literature predicts about behaviour and intergroup dynamics.

01 — Supervised learning · temporal validation

Predicting World Cup match outcomes from engineered pre-match strength features

Graduate data science coursework, Hebrew University of Jerusalem · Python 3.12 · scikit-learn

Two-person team project. Work was shared across analysis, evaluation and write-up.

Question

Can pre-match team-strength features predict whether a World Cup match ends in a Team A win, a draw, or a Team B win — and which characteristics actually carry the signal?

Data

45,816 international matches across 33 columns, spanning 1872–2026, compiled from FIFA's official tournament records. All matches were processed chronologically to build team histories; only World Cup finals matches were retained as modeling observations — 964 matches (1930–2022) for development and the first 90 matches of the 2026 tournament as an untouched out-of-time test set.

Target: three-class outcome, moderately imbalanced in development (57% Team A win, 18.6% draw, 24% Team B win). Knockout matches decided on penalties were coded to the shootout winner.

Feature engineering

The raw data contained no measure of relative team strength. Six predictors were constructed from match history alone:

  • Rolling Elo ratings computed chronologically over all 45,816 matches, initialized at 1,500, with competition-specific K factors (60 for World Cup finals, 40–50 for major tournaments, 20 default) and a goal-margin multiplier. elo_diff is the pre-match difference.
  • Recent form: difference in average points per game and average goal difference across each team's previous 10 matches.
  • Head-to-head: smoothed historical result rate, (score + 1)/(meetings + 2), to avoid extreme estimates from few meetings, plus the meeting count as an evidence indicator.
  • Tournament context: knockout-stage indicator, since draw frequency differs sharply by stage (24.4% in group play vs 1.6% in knockouts).

Every feature was recorded before its own match updated the historical record, so no outcome could influence its own predictors.

Validation

Expanding-window temporal cross-validation across five folds, each holding out a complete World Cup (2006, 2010, 2014, 2018, 2022) and predicting it using only earlier tournaments. Random k-fold was explicitly rejected: it would train on later tournaments to predict earlier ones, which neither reflects the real prediction setting nor prevents temporal leakage.

Metrics: macro-F1 as primary, plus accuracy, balanced accuracy, draw recall, and multiclass log-loss — the last because the intended output is a probability for decision support, not a hard label.

Both classifiers used balanced class weighting to counteract the low base rate of draws: standardized logistic regression (C=1.0) and a random forest of 400 trees with a minimum of 10 samples per leaf. Permutation importance was averaged over 30 repeats.

Findings

Logistic regression matched random forest on cross-validation (0.494 vs 0.496) but was more stable across folds and better on every out-of-time test metric. An explicit overfitting check explained why: random forest reached 0.638 macro-F1 in training against 0.496 in validation, a gap of 0.142, while logistic regression showed no training advantage at all (0.459 vs 0.494).

An ablation produced the central negative result. Elo alone achieved 0.490; Elo plus both recent-form variables scored lower at 0.469; all six features together reached 0.494 — an improvement of 0.004 over Elo alone, well inside fold-to-fold variation. Recent form, head-to-head history, and tournament stage added no measurable predictive value beyond Elo.

The descriptive gradient behind that result is clean: with a pre-match Elo gap under 50 points the favourite won 48% of matches, rising to 63% at 101–200 points and 70% above 200.

On calibration: the model was correct on 84.4% of the 32 matches where its top predicted probability reached 0.55, against 46.6% on the remaining 58. Draw detection improved markedly over the baselines (65.0% recall) but at 37% precision, so draw probability is usable as a close-match risk signal, not as a draw prediction.

What this demonstrates
  • Validation design matched to the prediction problem — expanding-window temporal folds and a held-out future tournament, with random k-fold explicitly ruled out and the reason documented.
  • Domain feature engineering from raw event data — implementing a sequential rating system over 45,816 records, with smoothing chosen to handle sparse matchup history.
  • Diagnosing model behavior, not just ranking models — quantified train/validation gaps, permutation importance, and a feature ablation that overturned the project's own hypothesis.
  • Probabilistic output translated into decision rules — confidence-thresholded accuracy and precision/recall trade-offs stated in operational terms.

02 — NLP · information retrieval · unsupervised learning

Comparing lexical and embedding-based retrieval, and clustering news documents

Graduate data science coursework, Hebrew University of Jerusalem · Python · gensim, rank_bm25, scikit-learn, NLTK

Two-person team project. Work was shared across analysis, evaluation and write-up.

Question

How do lexical retrieval (TF-IDF, BM25) and distributional-semantic retrieval (Word2Vec) differ in what they return for short queries — and do unsupervised embedding clusters recover the editorial categories of a news corpus without seeing the labels?

Data

BBC Full Text corpus: 2,225 articles across five categories (sport 511, business 510, politics 417, tech 401, entertainment 386). Preprocessing: lowercasing, non-alphabetic removal, whitespace tokenization, NLTK stop-word removal, minimum token length of three. Mean document length after cleaning: 212.5 tokens. Stemming and lemmatization were deliberately skipped so that all three retrieval methods received identical input and Word2Vec retained surface forms.

Methods
  • Retrieval: TF-IDF with cosine similarity, BM25 (Okapi), and a skip-gram Word2Vec model trained on the corpus (100 dimensions, window 5, minimum count 5, 10 epochs), with documents and queries represented as mean in-vocabulary word vectors.
  • Clustering: K-Means over L2-normalized mean Word2Vec document vectors, evaluated for K = 3–6 by silhouette score, with category labels withheld during fitting and selection.
  • Supervised reference: one-vs-rest multiclass logistic regression on TF-IDF features, with TF-IDF refitted inside each training fold.
Data quality

A full-text audit found 98 exact duplicate pairs in the supplied corpus. Rather than ignoring them, the supervised evaluation used stratified group k-fold cross-validation with identical texts assigned to the same group, so duplicate copies could not appear in both training and validation. The duplicates also explained an artifact in retrieval: one query returned the same article in both top-two positions.

Findings

BM25 was the most consistent retriever, winning two of three queries. TF-IDF was strongest when literal query terms matched intent, but retrieved a rugby article for a football query on the strength of shared terms like "match" and "goal." Word2Vec captured broader semantic relationships — retrieving a motion-sensor phone article for a technology query without the word "technology" appearing — at the cost of query specificity, since vector averaging tends toward general topic rather than precise information need.

The supervised reference reached 97% mean cross-validated accuracy (SD 0.8 pp), establishing that the categories are strongly lexically separable and giving the unsupervised result something to be measured against.

What this demonstrates
  • Text representation across paradigms — sparse lexical weighting, probabilistic ranking, and trained dense embeddings, compared on identical preprocessing.
  • Unsupervised evaluation done honestly — labels withheld during fitting and K selection, then used only for post-hoc interpretation, with a weak silhouette reported next to strong purity.
  • Detecting a data-quality problem and correcting the evaluation for it — duplicate-aware group cross-validation rather than an inflated accuracy figure.
  • Qualitative error analysis — diagnosing why each method failed on specific queries, not only which scored higher.

03 — Classification · API data construction

Predicting Nobel Prize category from biography and education history

Graduate data science coursework (Course 55810), Hebrew University of Jerusalem · Python · scikit-learn, statsmodels

Summary

Six-class classification over 990 Nobel laureates (1901–2025), testing whether educational mobility — how far a laureate studied from their birthplace — predicts prize category beyond demographics.

Education history did not exist in the source data. It was built by querying the Wikidata API for each laureate's institutions with session-level retry and backoff, resolving those institutions to coordinates, and computing great-circle distances. Skew was handled by median imputation, 95th-percentile winsorization, and log transformation — implemented as a custom scikit-learn transformer so those statistics were learned only within each training fold.

What this demonstrates
  • Constructing a dataset that did not exist — programmatic API retrieval, entity resolution, and geographic feature construction from an external knowledge base.
  • Leakage control written by hand — a custom transformer implementing BaseEstimator and TransformerMixin so preprocessing statistics never cross the train/test boundary.
  • Reporting a hypothesis that failed, with class-level diagnostics identifying exactly where and why.

Practice

AI-enabled research workflow

I use frontier AI models as working instruments across the research process, not as a novelty. In practice that means coding assistance and debugging during analysis, literature synthesis, structuring multi-stage research workflows, and iterating on prompts until the output is reliable enough to build on.

The part I treat most carefully is evaluating model output — checking claims against sources, catching confident errors, and knowing which tasks a model can be trusted with and which it cannot. Working with these systems on quantitative research has made me a sharp reader of where they break down, which is itself the relevant skill for evaluation work.

This describes applied use of AI systems in research practice. It is not professional machine learning engineering experience.
